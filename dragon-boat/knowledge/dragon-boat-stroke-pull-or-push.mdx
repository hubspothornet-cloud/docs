## Direct Answer

In Pat Bradley's coaching framework, the drive phase is taught as pressure and push rather than an outside-arm pull. The preferred sequence is immediate downward pressure at paddle entry, followed by leg drive and sitting up through the stroke.

The objective is to connect the paddle to the body's larger muscle groups instead of allowing the biceps and outside arm to dominate the movement.

<Note>
  This guide is based on a Coach's Corner article by Pat Bradley, identified in the original publication as a World Champion National Team and Club Crew Coach. The original article was published by Hornet Watersports on June 13, 2024.
</Note>

## Why the Word "Pull" Can Create a Problem

When newer paddlers are told to pull, they may bend the outside arm early and try to move the paddle primarily with the biceps.

Bradley's concern is that an arm-dominant pull can reduce the transfer of force from the body to the paddle and can weaken the paddle-entry position.

The coaching cue changes when the paddler thinks about pressure and push:

- Pressure directs attention to the paddle entry and connection with the water.
- Leg drive brings larger muscle groups into the movement.
- Sitting up connects the lower body, trunk, and paddle through the drive.
- The outside arm supports the structure rather than becoming the main power source.

## Bradley's Two Technical Priorities

| Sequence | Coaching priority | Intended result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Apply immediate downward pressure with the body at paddle entry | Establish a connected catch and positive entry angle |
| 2 | Push through the legs and sit up through the drive | Transfer power from larger muscle groups into the paddle |

Bradley teaches these movements separately at first so the athlete can identify each body action. Once both movements are understood, they are combined and repeated to develop a consistent pattern.

## A Simple Learning Progression

### 1. Isolate the Catch Pressure

Begin in the paddle-entry position and focus only on creating immediate downward pressure through the body.

The goal is not to pull the handle backward with the outside arm. The goal is to establish connection before the rest of the drive develops.

### 2. Isolate Leg Drive and Sitting Up

Practice the lower-body push and the movement of sitting up without rushing into an arm pull.

The paddler should feel the legs and trunk contributing to the movement rather than waiting for the arms to do the work.

### 3. Combine the Movements

Link the downward pressure at entry with the leg drive and body movement that follows.

The sequence should feel connected rather than like separate arm, trunk, and leg actions.

### 4. Repeat Until the Cue Becomes Automatic

Bradley's teaching approach separates the movements first, then combines them to build muscle memory.

## Common Signs of an Arm-Dominant Pull

A paddler may be relying too much on the outside arm when:

- The elbow bends immediately after paddle entry
- The catch loses pressure before the body begins to move
- The paddle is moved mainly by the biceps
- The legs contribute little to the drive
- The body collapses forward instead of sitting up through the stroke
- The paddle feels disconnected from the paddler's larger muscle groups

These signs are useful observation points. Teams may use different language and stroke models, so the same cue may not produce the same result for every paddler.

## How This Fits With Team Technique

Dragon boat teams can use different entry styles, stroke lengths, rates, and recovery patterns. Bradley's central point is not that every team must use identical words. It is that the drive should remain connected to the body and should not become an arm-dominant pull.

A team may describe the movement as pressure, drive, push, connection, or another cue. The useful question is whether the cue produces a strong catch and coordinated use of the legs and trunk.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should a dragon boat paddler pull with the outside arm?

The outside arm is part of the stroke structure, but Bradley's framework warns against making the biceps and outside arm the primary source of power. He emphasizes downward pressure, leg drive, and connected body movement.

### What happens first in this stroke sequence?

Immediate downward pressure at the paddle entry comes first. Leg drive and sitting up follow as the stroke develops.

### Why teach the movements separately?

Separating the catch pressure from the leg-and-body drive helps the athlete identify each movement before combining them into one coordinated sequence.

### Is "push" the only correct coaching word?

No single term works identically for every paddler or team. In Bradley's framework, push and pressure are useful because they reduce the tendency to make the stroke an arm-dominant pull.

### Does this technique apply only to elite paddlers?

No. The original article specifically notes that newer paddlers may respond to the word pull by overusing the outside arm, so the framework is relevant during early technique development as well as higher-level coaching.
